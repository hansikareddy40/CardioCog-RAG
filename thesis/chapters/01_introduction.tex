\section{Background and Motivation}

The changes in the brain that lead to dementia build up over years, and many people pass through a middle stage, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), in which memory or thinking is measurably weaker but daily life is still managed independently. Some people stay in that stage for a long time, some return to normal, and some go on to dementia within a few years. Even among people who seem cognitively normal, a small number decline quickly. If clinicians could tell, early and reliably, who is heading towards decline, they could follow those people more closely, plan care with the family, and select the right participants for clinical trials.

Much of the information needed for such an estimate is already collected in memory clinics. Age is the strongest single risk factor. Cardiovascular and metabolic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes are associated with later dementia in large epidemiological studies \cite{livingston2020,sierra2020,hainsworth2024,cao2024}. The APOE $\varepsilon$4 allele is the main genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's disease \cite{wolfe2018}. Neuropsychological tests and clinical ratings describe how a person is thinking and functioning today, and amyloid and tau PET imaging show the underlying pathology directly \cite{chandra2019,leuzy2019}. What remains unclear is how much each of these sources really adds once the others are known, and whether complex machine-learning models are needed to combine them.

This project, CardioCog, set out to build an explainable framework that assesses cognitive health and predicts cognitive decline from cardiovascular, genetic and clinical information, with PET where it is available, and to support each prediction with published evidence.

\section{How the Research Question Developed}

The project began with a broad aim: to combine cardiovascular risk factors, cognitive trajectories, APOE and PET biomarkers to predict cognitive decline, and to explain the predictions with supporting literature. Before any model was trained, the data were audited in detail. Three findings from that audit determined the design of the study. First, the clinical diagnosis and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) are recorded at every visit across twenty years of data, whereas the neuropsychological battery changed in 2015. Second, roughly one in three participants with MCI progresses to dementia within three years, so a three-year window contains enough events to learn from. Third, most PET scans are recent, and only a few hundred participants have raw images, of whom very few have declined since.

``Cognitive decline'' was therefore made concrete in two complementary ways, both measured over three years from a clinic visit. The primary outcome is progression to a clinical diagnosis of dementia. The secondary outcome is a worsening of at least one point in the CDR Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), which also captures people who decline without reaching dementia; its continuous counterpart, the change in CDR-SB, is used for a progression estimate. The original cardiovascular hypothesis was kept, but as a question to be tested rather than an assumption.

\subsection{The First-Review Prototype}
\label{sec:prelim}
The work shown at the first review followed the original imaging plan closely. A 3D CNN on raw amyloid PET volumes was fused with 34 clinical features through spatial cross-attention, to predict a rise of at least 0.5 in CDR-SB after one year. Only 198 participants had both a usable image and a one-year outcome, and only 17 of them progressed (8.6\%). Under five-fold cross-validation the fused model reached a mean AUROC of 0.786 (standard deviation 0.134) in the run reported at the review, with fold values between 0.55 and 0.96. A later run of the same notebook gave 0.652 (standard deviation 0.153), with fold values between 0.40 and 0.86. Tabular models on the same participants reached at most 0.78, again with standard deviations above 0.1.

When two runs of one design differ by more than 0.13 AUROC, no conclusion can be drawn from the result; with 17 events, a few participants determine the score. A review of the prototype also found technical problems. Slices of dynamic PET acquisitions were ordered by position only, so different time frames could be mixed in one volume; no spatial registration was applied; one cardiovascular variable was read from a column that does not exist in the release; and the SHAP analysis was fitted on all participants rather than on unseen ones. These problems led to the design used in this thesis: the full clinical cohort, three-year outcomes with thousands of events, and a separate, validated treatment of the PET images (Chapter~\ref{ch:petimages}).

\section{Problem Statement}

Let $\mathbf{x}_{t_0}$ be the information available about a person without dementia at a clinic visit $t_0$: demographics, cardiovascular and medical history, neuropsychological test results, the clinician's rating, genetics and, when available, PET measures. The problem is to estimate three quantities over the following three years:
\begin{align}
\hat{p}_{\text{dem}} &= P(\text{dementia diagnosed in } (t_0, t_0+3\,\text{y}] \mid \mathbf{x}_{t_0}), \\
\hat{p}_{\text{dec}} &= P(\text{CDR-SB rises by} \geq 1 \text{ in } (t_0, t_0+3\,\text{y}] \mid \mathbf{x}_{t_0}), \\
\hat{\Delta} &= E(\text{CDR-SB}_{t_0+3\,\text{y}} - \text{CDR-SB}_{t_0} \mid \mathbf{x}_{t_0}),
\end{align}
to determine how much each kind of information contributes, and to explain each estimate in terms a clinician can check, without using any information that would not have been available at $t_0$.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}

The study answers seven research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.},leftmargin=*]
\item How accurately can progression to dementia and cognitive decline within three years be predicted, and how does this differ between cognitively normal people and people with MCI?
\item Does cardiovascular and medical information improve the prediction beyond demographics, genetics and current cognition?
\item Do amyloid and tau PET measures improve the prediction?
\item Do deep-learning models, including a modality-encoder fusion network, outperform classical models on this kind of data?
\item Does information from earlier visits improve the prediction beyond the current visit?
\item Can one model give calibrated estimates when whole kinds of information are missing?
\item Can each estimate be explained and supported with published evidence in a form a clinician can read?
\end{enumerate}

The objectives were to build a leakage-free cohort and outcomes from the NACC database; to harmonise neuropsychological tests across form versions; to train and compare classical and deep models under a person-level split with held-out centres; to measure the contribution of each kind of information by ablation; to explain predictions with Shapley values and attention weights; to test the added value of PET and of earlier visits; to validate the models in subgroups, for calibration and in sensitivity analyses; to build and validate a PET image pipeline; to deliver a clinician-facing prototype with a plain-language, evidence-backed report; and to verify the integrity of the entire pipeline with independent checks.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
\item A reproducible pipeline that turns the NACC database into leakage-free three-year prediction tasks for dementia and for cognitive decline, with neuropsychological tests harmonised across form versions.
\item A controlled comparison of classical models, deep tabular models and a modality-encoder fusion network, showing that model complexity brings no gain on these data.
\item A measured answer to the cardiovascular question: once current cognition is known, cardiovascular and medical history adds about 0.001 AUROC for dementia and nothing measurable for CDR-SB decline.
\item Results reported separately for cognitively normal participants and participants with MCI, with a quantification of how strongly the predictions depend on current cognitive status.
\item A PET image pipeline whose amyloid measurements agree with the PET core's (correlation 0.98).
\item A research prototype that checks the plausibility of its inputs and writes a plain-language report with quoted evidence.
\item Seventeen independent verification checks, which found and corrected two sources of information leakage.
\end{itemize}
